CHAPTER TWO — THE BODY


                              I.
                    THE HOTEL MISKATONIC


For a long moment after the student left, nobody moved.

The news sat in the room like smoke. Fairchild's face had gone the color
of old paper. Bill was on his feet, though he did not remember standing.
Nell's pen had stopped moving. Mikel, who had understood the word dead
without needing to understand anything else, stood at the window and
watched the rain and said nothing, because he could not.

Then they talked. Not well, and not for long. The conversation had the
ragged, overlapping quality of people who were frightened and trying not
to show it — voices talking over voices, half-formed plans colliding and
breaking apart.

Bill wanted the body. "We need to see him. We need to see what happened."

Sean wanted the room. "If Vic was working a case, his notes are in that
hotel room. If someone got to him, they'll be cleaning up. The room
won't wait."

Both were right. Neither could do both.

It was Nell who cut through it. "Then we split up. Bill, Fairchild,
Mikel, and I go to the river. Sean, you take the hotel. We meet after."

Sean nodded. He left Warren Hall alone, on foot, heading south on
College Street toward the Hotel Miskatonic.


                              *


The Hotel Miskatonic stood on College Street, directly across from the
Orne Library — a four-story brick building with pretensions to
respectability that the Depression had quietly revoked. In better days
it had been a commercial hotel — salesmen, visiting lecturers, the
occasional touring company. Now it was the kind of place that asked no
questions because the answers would only create complications.

Sean stood on the opposite side of the street and looked at it.

At first he missed it. The building presented itself the way buildings
do — a lobby entrance, a service door around the side, windows going up
four floors to a penthouse level. Ordinary. Anonymous. The kind of place
you walked into and walked out of and forgot about.

He looked again.

The men at the front entrance. Two of them. They were not doormen and
they were not loitering. They stood the way men stand when standing in a
particular spot is their job — weight distributed, eyes moving,
positioned to see the street in both directions. They wore overcoats that
were too heavy for May. One of them had his hands in his pockets in a way
that was not casual.

Sean's eyes went up. The penthouse. A figure in the window — just a
shape, a silhouette against lamplight, but the posture was wrong for a
guest. The posture was proprietary. A man looking down at his street from
his building.

It hit him the way a gear engages — one mechanism locking into another
with a click you could feel in your teeth. Irish mob. The overcoats, the
positioning, the way the two at the door tracked every car that passed.
This was not a hotel that happened to have security. This was an
operation. And the man in the penthouse — whoever he was — was running it.

Sean did not know the name. He did not need to. He had grown up inside
this machinery. He knew its shape by feel, the way a blind man knows a
room. Every bootlegging territory he had ever worked had a building like
this — the hub, the headquarters, the place where the boss slept above
his own supply line and the muscle stood at the doors and the whole
enterprise ticked along behind a facade of legitimate commerce. He had
been this kind of muscle himself, in Detroit, before the Purple Gang had
carved their opinion of his employment into his face.

Vic Valentine had been staying in a mob hotel. Had Vic known? Had
Fairchild?

Sean crossed the street.


                              *


The front door was the wrong play, but Sean tried it anyway. He walked
up with his collar turned and his hands visible and the particular
unhurried stride of a man who belonged. He did not belong. The enforcers
made him in three seconds. It was the scar. In a town full of students
and professors and Depression-era drifters, a man with a Glasgow smile
was a man with a history, and the history was the wrong kind.

"Help you?" The taller one. Irish accent thick enough to pour.

"Visiting a friend. Got a room upstairs."

"That so." It was not a question. "You armed?"

"No."

A pause. The kind of pause that is a sentence in itself.

"We're going to need to pat you down, then."

Sean looked at the hand reaching toward his coat, and at the Colt .38
Super riding under it, and at the very simple arithmetic of the
situation. He could bluff and get frisked and lose the gun. He could
refuse and escalate. Or he could tell the truth, which in this case was
the cheapest option.

"Alright. I'm carrying."

The taller enforcer's expression did not change. It simply closed, the
way a shop closes at the end of the day — the openness replaced by
something flat and final.

"Fuck off."

"I've got a friend upstairs. Name of Valentine. Just need to —"

"Fuck him and fuck you too. Now fuck off."

Sean fucked off.


                              *


He circled the building. The alley on the east side was narrow and wet
and smelled of garbage and the river. The back entrance was a service
door — heavy, metal, propped open with a brick. One man. Younger than the
front pair, thicker, standing with the bored posture of a man pulling
the shift nobody wanted.

Sean waited.

Patience was a skill that the bootlegging trade taught early and
thoroughly. You learned to wait in cars on dark roads while customs men
made their rounds. You learned to wait at docks while the tide shifted.
You learned that the man who moved too soon was the man who got caught,
and the man who got caught was the man who got hurt, and hurt in this
business meant a shallow grave or a Glasgow smile or both.

Sean waited in the alley, in the rain, pressed against the brick, and
after seven minutes the back-door man stepped inside. Bathroom break, or
a conversation, or just the restlessness of a man who had been standing
in the rain long enough. It didn't matter. The doorway was empty.

Sean moved.

He came through the service entrance fast and quiet and found the man
three steps inside, his back turned, and hit him once with the butt of
the Colt — hard, behind the ear, with the practiced economy of a man
who understood the difference between enough force and too much. The
enforcer went down like a bag of sand. Sean caught him under the arms
and eased him against the wall and took his pulse — steady, strong,
unconscious — and went up the back stairs without looking back.

The hotel's interior was a warren of dim hallways and closed doors and
the mingled smells of cigar smoke, floor polish, and something sweet and
chemical that might have been cleaning solution and might have been
something else. Sean moved through it the way water moves through pipes
— naturally, without drawing attention, following the path of least
resistance. The staff saw a man in an overcoat moving with purpose and
did not stop him, because men in overcoats moving with purpose were
the Hotel Miskatonic's primary clientele. The gangsters, if there were
gangsters on the upper floors, did not see him at all.

He found Vic's room on the third floor. A door like every other door.
Locked.

Sean tried the handle. Solid. He stepped back and put his shoulder into
it.

The door held. Pain blossomed through his left shoulder and down into
his arm, the bright sharp pain of something strained or torn. He tried
again — harder, angrier, the way a man hits a thing when hitting it has
become personal — and the door held again and the pain in his shoulder
became the kind of pain that argues with you, that says stop, that makes
its case in a language older than words.

The door was not going to open.

Sean stood in the hallway with his shoulder throbbing and his jaw set
and the cold, clear understanding of a man who knew when a job had gone
sideways settling into his chest like a stone. The room was there. Vic's
notes were in it. The answers might be on the other side of one locked
door and he could not get through it.

He went back the way he had come. Down the stairs, through the service
corridor, past the unconscious enforcer — still breathing, still out —
and into the alley and the rain. His shoulder ached in a way that was
not going to improve soon. He walked back to the side street near campus
where he had left the Studebaker, and drove toward the river.


                              II.
                    THE BODY THAT WASN'T THERE


The docks were a confusion of mud and rope and the smell of the river.

Bill, Nell, Mikel, and Professor Fairchild arrived to find an absence
where a body should have been. The police had come and gone. The crowd
that gathers at the site of a death — the particular, guilty crowd that
materializes around tragedy the way flies materialize around fruit —
had thinned to a handful of dockworkers and an old man standing at the
waterline with his hands in his pockets, staring at the current as
though it owed him something.

Vic Valentine's body had been pulled from the Miskatonic an hour ago.
It was already at the county morgue.

The rain fell. The river ran.

Nell went to work.

She moved among the dockworkers with the casual authority of a woman who
had spent a decade extracting information from people who did not want to
give it. Her notepad was out. Her pen moved. The dockworkers, tired and
wet and wary in the way that men who work the waterfront are always wary
of questions, gave her what they had.

A local man named Steven White had found the body. Saw it caught against
the pilings beneath the bridge, snagged on something, turning slowly in
the current. White had called for help. The police had come. They had
taken the body and they had taken White, and that was all anyone knew.

While Nell talked, Bill went under the bridge.

He was a tracker. It was the thing he could do — the one skill that had
followed him from the open country of west Texas through the trenches of
France and into the cramped, incomprehensible streets of New England. He
could read ground. And the ground beneath the Garrison Street Bridge was
telling him something that did not match the story.

Drag marks. In the mud, leading from the bank down to the water's edge.
Deep, parallel furrows — the kind made by something heavy being pulled,
not pushed, not rolled, but dragged by men who were working quickly and
did not care about the marks they left. The furrows led to the river.
Not from it.

Someone had brought a body to this spot and put it in the water. The
river had not delivered Vic Valentine. Someone had delivered Vic
Valentine to the river.

Bill climbed back up the bank and said nothing. He filed it the way he
filed everything — quietly, in a place where it would keep until he
needed it.

Nell found something too. A scrap of fabric snagged on a nail beneath
the bridge railing — dark wool, heavy weave, the kind of cloth you'd
find in a man's overcoat. Torn, as if ripped free in haste. She folded
it into her notepad and wrote the word WOOL in her shorthand and
underlined it twice.

Mikel searched the bank. He found nothing. The river and the rain had
taken whatever else there might have been to find, and his skills — the
navigator's eye, the sailor's instinct for reading the shape of water
and land — gave him nothing here that the mud and the darkness did not
immediately take back.

The sound of an engine. Sean's Studebaker rolled to a stop at the edge
of the dock road, and Sean got out, moving his left shoulder carefully
in a way that Nell noticed and did not remark upon.

"The room's locked," Sean said. "Couldn't get in." He did not mention
the mob. He did not mention the penthouse. He would, later, when they
were not standing in the open.

Nell looked at the group — four investigators and one professor, standing
in the rain at the edge of a river that had given up a dead man and
nothing else — and said the thing that was obvious.

"The morgue. If the body's there, that's where the answers are."

They got in the car.


                              III.
                    THE CORONER'S LIE


The county coroner's office occupied the basement level of the Arkham
municipal building — a stone structure in the administrative center of
town that housed the police department, the records office, and, in its
lowest and least visited reaches, the morgue. The stairs going down were
narrow and smelled of formaldehyde and damp concrete and something else,
something sweet and wrong, the smell that clings to places where the
dead are stored and examined and released to whatever comes next.

Rutherford Pike met them at the bottom of the stairs.

He was a thin man, precise, with the careful bearing of a person who
has spent his career in the company of bodies and has learned to hold
himself at a permanent slight distance from everything, as though the
whole of the living world were a specimen he had not yet been asked to
classify. He wore a white coat. His hands were clean. His eyes, behind
steel-rimmed spectacles, were the eyes of a man who was doing his best
to look like a man who had nothing to hide.

Nell saw through it the way light passes through glass — not by effort
but by nature. She led the questioning.

"Mr. Valentine was brought in this morning. We're his associates."

Pike's expression arranged itself into the particular shape of
professional sympathy. "Yes. A tragedy. The gentleman was recovered from
the river this morning. He appears to have been in the water for some
time — weeks, most likely. The cause of death, as best I can determine,
is blunt force trauma consistent with prolonged immersion in a river
with significant current. Debris. Rocks. The Miskatonic is not gentle
with what it carries."

Nell's pen moved. "Weeks."

"At minimum."

"And the injuries are consistent with the river. Rocks. Debris."

"Entirely consistent." Pike's hands were in the pockets of his white
coat. They had been there since the beginning of the conversation and
they had not come out. A man with nothing to hide does not hide his
hands. "The damage to the face and head is severe but not inconsistent
with what one sees in drowning victims recovered from moving water.
The Miskatonic — "

"Are you fucking stupid?"

Bill's voice filled the basement the way a gunshot fills a room — sudden,
percussive, leaving a silence in its wake that was louder than the sound
itself. He had been standing at the back of the group, large and quiet,
and now he was not quiet anymore.

Pike's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

"Weeks in the river," Bill said. He took a step forward. "Blunt force
from debris. You're standing there in your little coat with your hands
in your pockets telling us that a man we know — a man who was alive days
ago — has been dead for weeks. You think we can't count?"

Pike took a step back.

"Sir, I must ask you to —"

Bill pushed past him.

The doors to the examination room were not locked. Bill went through
them the way he went through most obstacles — directly, without
hesitation, with the blunt physical certainty of a man for whom doors
were suggestions rather than barriers. Pike turned and ran.

Mikel moved. He saw Pike heading for the stairs — heading for the police,
heading for authority, heading for the thing that would end whatever time
they had with the body — and he tried to cut him off. He stepped into
Pike's path with the sudden, looming silence of a man who could not say
stop but whose body said it clearly. Pike did not stop. Pike was too
fast, too frightened, too motivated by whatever was driving him to
maintain his lie, and he slipped past Mikel and up the stairs, his
white coat flapping behind him like a surrender flag being withdrawn.

They had minutes. Maybe less.


                              *


The examination room was cold and white and smelled of things that Nell
would remember for the rest of her life.

Vic Valentine lay on a steel table under a sheet that someone had
pulled up to his chin with the impersonal efficiency of a clerk filing
a document. Sean pulled the sheet back.

Fairchild made a sound. Not a word — a sound, low and involuntary, the
kind of noise a man makes when something inside him breaks before his
mind can intervene. He stumbled back a step. His hand found the edge of
a counter and gripped it, and his face went from pale to grey to the
particular shade of green that precedes vomiting. He turned away. His
shoulders heaved once, twice. He did not vomit, but it was a near thing,
and when he turned back his eyes were wet and his mouth was a thin, white
line and he looked, for the first time since they had met him, like a
man who had reached the end of something.

This was his friend. This had been his friend.

For a moment, no one else spoke.

His eyes had burst.

That was the first thing. The sockets were dark ruins — not empty, not
damaged in the way that water and rocks damage a body, but destroyed
from the inside, as if something behind the eyes had detonated. The
tissue was blackened. The lids were gone.

His face was burned. Not blistered, not reddened in the way of a man
who has been too close to a fire. Burned in a way that had no name Nell
could find in her vocabulary — the skin darkened and drawn tight across
the bones of the skull, cracked in places, the underlying tissue seared
as if by a heat that had come from everywhere and nowhere, that had no
source and no direction. It was not the damage of river debris. It was
not the damage of anything that belonged in the world that Rutherford
Pike described in his careful, lying voice.

Whatever had killed Vic Valentine, it had started with his face.

They worked fast. Four pairs of hands on a dead man's body, looking for
what Pike had tried to hide. Sean's hands moved with a steadiness that
came from somewhere Nell did not ask about. Nell catalogued the visible
injuries with the clinical precision of a woman who had trained herself
to see clearly in conditions that demanded she look away. Bill tried to
read the body the way he read ground — looking for signs, for a
narrative in the damage — and found nothing he could parse. The injuries
were beyond his vocabulary.

Mikel.

Mikel, who could not ask questions and could not read reports and could
not discuss his findings with the others in any language they shared,
put his hands on the dead man's neck and jaw and skull with the careful,
exploratory touch of a man who had spent his life fixing things with his
hands and understood, through touch, what his eyes could not fully
comprehend. He turned the head. He felt the bones. He pressed, gently,
along the jaw and the temples and the ridge of the brow.

And he knew.

The damage to the face was catastrophic — the burst eyes, the burns,
the destruction — but it was not what had killed him. The body beneath
the ruined face was intact. No broken bones. No crushing injuries. No
evidence of the kind of sustained violence that kills a man through
accumulated trauma. Whatever had happened to Vic Valentine's face had
happened all at once, and it had been so sudden and so agonizing that
his body had simply stopped. The shock — the searing, incomprehensible
pain of his own face burning and his eyes bursting in their sockets —
had overwhelmed every system that kept him alive.

He had died of shock. From the horror and the pain of whatever had been
done to him.

Mikel looked up from the body. He could not say this. He had no words
for it in English. He touched his own face, then his chest — heart —
then made a gesture with both hands that was unmistakable in any
language: finished. Over. Gone.

Sean understood first. "Shock," he said quietly. "He died of shock."

Mikel nodded once.

The basement door opened. Footsteps on the stairs. More than two pairs.

Pike's voice, high and agitated: "That one. The big one. He assaulted me
and forced his way in. I want him arrested."

That one. The big one. Not them. Him.

Pike's finger, shaking and precise, pointed at Bill O'Job and no one
else.


                              IV.
                        THE COWBOY AND THE COPS


Two uniformed officers came down the stairs behind Pike. They were young
and they were armed and they had the particular, uncertain energy of men
who had been told a story and were trying to decide, in real time,
whether it warranted the authority they were about to exercise. Their
eyes followed Pike's finger to Bill, and in that moment the rest of the
room became invisible.

Nell saw the opening and took it. She had the journalist's instinct for
the precise moment when a situation shifts from story to danger, and
this was that moment. She went for the stairs while the officers' eyes
were on Bill. Sean was a step behind her — a man who had grown up in
rooms where the police arrived and who understood, at a cellular level,
that the correct response to police arriving was to not be in the room
when they did. Fairchild followed, grey-faced and trembling, a professor
in a world that had suddenly stopped resembling a university.

"Alright," the taller officer said, his eyes on Bill. "You. Step away
from the body."

Mikel stayed.

He stood between Bill and the officers with his hands raised, palms
out, and tried to communicate the thing he could not say — that this was
a misunderstanding, that no one needed to be arrested, that the dead man
on the table was their friend and the coroner was lying and if everyone
would just stop for one moment and look at what was actually in front of
them, the truth was right there, right there on the steel table, in the
ruined face of a man who had not died in any river.

He used his hands. He used his face. He used the full vocabulary of
gesture that a lifetime of working across language barriers had given him
— the open palms, the lowered shoulders, the slow head-shake, the
universal grammar of a man trying to say peace.

Nobody was listening.

"Sir." The officer's hand was on his holster. "Step aside. Now."

Mikel stepped aside.

Bill did not.

"This is horseshit," Bill said. His voice was quiet but it was the
quiet of a wire under tension, the quiet that precedes the snap. "Your
coroner is lying to you. That man on the table didn't drown and he
wasn't in any river for weeks and if you'd take thirty seconds to look
at the body instead of taking his word for it —"

"Sir, you need to come with us."

"I'm not going anywhere until someone looks at —"

"Sir."

And then — because Bill O'Job was a man who had been broke and hungry and
pushed around for long enough that the mechanism inside him that
distinguished between righteous anger and self-destruction had worn
smooth and stopped working — he swung.

The first punch caught the nearer officer on the jaw. Bill's fist
connected with the blunt, unambiguous authority of a man who had learned
to fight in places where fighting was not a sport but a means of
continuing to exist. The cop went sideways. His partner lunged.

Bill dodged. The second officer's grab sailed past his shoulder and
closed on air. For a moment the three of them were frozen in a tableau
— Bill in the center, the officers on either side, the basement bright
with fluorescent light and loud with the echoing aftermath of a punch
that had changed everything.

Then the second cop connected. Not a punch — a grab, a tackle, the
full-body lunge of a man who understood that the situation had passed
the point where words were useful. He got Bill around the midsection and
drove him into the wall. The first officer, jaw reddening, joined him.

Mikel tried one more time. He stepped forward with his hands up, making
the gesture that means wait, that means stop, that means there is a
better way to do this — and both officers and Bill ignored him with the
total, focused indifference of men who had committed to violence and
could not be recalled from it.

Mikel ran.

In the basement, the two officers pinned Bill against the wall with
everything they had. For a moment — a long, straining, grunting moment —
it held. The cops were younger and there were two of them and they had
the mechanical advantage of position, pressing Bill's chest against the
concrete, his arms wrenched behind him.

Bill had something they did not. He had eighty pounds of muscle built
in the open country of west Texas and maintained through a decade of
manual labor and deprivation, and he had the particular, desperate
strength of a man who had been held down before — by poverty, by the
war, by the Depression, by every institution that had ever looked at
him and decided he was not worth the trouble — and who had learned that
the only way out was through.

He surged. It was not technique. It was not skill. It was the brute,
animal refusal of a body that would not be restrained. He planted his
feet and drove upward and outward with everything he had, and the
officers — both of them, at the same time — came off him like riders
thrown from a horse.

For one second, Bill O'Job stood in the basement of the Arkham county
building, free, breathing hard, blood on his knuckles, and the two
officers were on the floor, and the fluorescent lights buzzed overhead
with the idiot indifference of electricity.

Then the first officer drew his .38.

The shot was perfect. A textbook draw, a steady aim, a trigger squeeze
that the officer's training sergeant would have commended. The bullet
hit Bill in the upper arm — and by every law of probability and
ballistics, it should have put him down. A .38 Special at close range
delivers enough force to shatter bone.

It grazed him.

One inch to the left and the bullet would have punched through muscle
and lodged against the humerus. One inch to the right and it would have
missed entirely. Instead it traced a shallow furrow along the outside
of his arm — painful, bloody, and by the most undeserved margin
imaginable, not serious. The kind of wound that hurts like hell and
heals in a week.

Bill did not go down.

The second shot went wide.

Bill was still moving — still fighting, still swinging, driven by an
engine that pain and reason could not reach. He caught the second
officer with another blow and dodged a third grab and for a few
terrible seconds the basement of the county building sounded like what
it was: a place where a man was fighting for something he did not fully
understand against an authority that did not fully understand him.

Then the reinforcements came. Drawn by the gunshot — by the impossible,
echoing crack of a firearm discharged in a concrete basement — more
officers, from upstairs, from the precinct, from wherever cops come
from when the sound of a gun rewrites the priorities of an afternoon.
Three of them. Four. Enough.

Bill went down.

Not easily. Not quietly. He went down the way a building goes down — in
stages, fighting gravity at every level, refusing the premise until the
premise became undeniable. They piled onto him. They wrenched his arms
behind his back. They put him on the floor.

Bill O'Job lay on the concrete with his face pressed against the cold
and his wounded arm bleeding through his shirt and the weight of four
Arkham police officers holding him in place, and the fluorescent lights
buzzed above him, and Vic Valentine's body lay on the steel table ten
feet away, eyes burst, face burned, dead from shock, and the county
coroner stood at the top of the stairs and watched with the expression
of a man whose lie had held — for now, at the cost of a shooting, but
held.

They put Bill in handcuffs. They read him something. They took him
upstairs.


                              *


Outside, in the rain, Nell and Sean and Fairchild stood on the sidewalk
and listened to the muffled sound of a gunshot echo through the
basement windows and did not go back inside.

Mikel came out a minute later, moving fast, his face set in the blank
expression that he wore when his inability to speak had become not a
limitation but a mercy — because what was there to say? What words, in
what language, could describe what they had seen on that table and what
had happened afterward?

The four of them stood in the rain. The Studebaker idled at the curb.
Somewhere inside the building, Bill O'Job was being arrested for
assaulting police officers who were protecting a coroner who was lying
about the murder of their friend.

Nell opened her notepad. The rain hit the page and the ink from her
earlier notes began to bleed, the words dissolving into blue-grey
ghosts of themselves.

She wrote: EYES BURST. FACE BURNED FROM WITHIN. PIKE LIED.

She closed the notepad.

"Now what?" Sean said.

Nobody answered. The rain fell. The Studebaker idled. Inside the
municipal building, the machinery of Arkham law enforcement was
processing Bill O'Job into a cell, and the body of Vic Valentine was
being covered again by a sheet pulled up by the clean, careful hands of
a coroner whose story — weeks in the water, blunt force trauma, nothing
suspicious — would go into the official record and stay there.

Nell looked at Sean. Sean looked at Fairchild. Fairchild looked at his
shoes.

Mikel looked at the building.

None of them knew what to do next. None of them had expected this.
They had come to Arkham following a dead man's letter, and in the space
of a single afternoon they had lost the body to a lie, lost their
strongest fighter to a jail cell, and gained nothing but questions —
questions about burst eyes and burned faces and coroners who lied and
hotel rooms guarded by Irish gangsters and a detective who had been
murdered by something that did not belong in any world that Rutherford
Pike would ever write about in an official report.

The rain fell.

It had been raining for four days now.

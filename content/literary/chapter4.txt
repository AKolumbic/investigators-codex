CHAPTER FOUR — THE KING IN SHADOW


                              I.
                    THE CHURCH ON THE HILL


By the time Nell Carroway caught up with the others, the day had already
acquired the feeling of one that intended to go bad in public.

Arkham had that quality sometimes. A pressure in the air. Not weather,
though the weather helped — a low grey sky over the university, the brick
buildings holding damp like old men held grudges, and the spring wind
coming off the river with just enough bite left in it to keep kindness out
of people's faces. But beyond the weather there was another thing, less
nameable and more useful, which was that the city seemed to have made a
decision without telling anyone what it was.

Nell found them not far from the Orne Library: Sean with his coat collar
up and his injured shoulder making him meaner than usual, Mikel quiet as
ever and watching the street instead of the people on it, and Aiden Chez
standing with the easy patience of a man who preferred to let a scene
arrange itself before stepping into the middle of it.

Nell wasted no time.

She told them about June St. Clair. The Gilded Swan. The special stock
moving through the cheaper joints near the docks. Men who drank it and
came apart by degrees. Not O'Bannion's usual supply. Something else.

Then she gave them the name.

The King in Shadow.

The words landed on each of them differently.

On Sean they landed like a threat from a trade he recognized too well. Men
did not acquire names like that by being harmless, and they did not keep
them by accident. On Mikel the words landed as sound before meaning,
another English shape he had to fit into a growing cabinet of dangerous
ones: Vic, Ashburn, Pike, hotel, dead. On Aiden they landed as structure.
Not proof. Not yet. But structure. Names gave cases somewhere to hang
their weight.

"If Arkham has a devil," Aiden said, "some priest has been asked about him."

That turned them away from the library and up toward St. Stanislaus.

It was a Polish church on the rise east of campus, brick and stone and
stubbornness, built by men who had crossed an ocean and then discovered
that New England intended to remain New England whether anyone wanted it to
or not. The front steps were damp. Candles glowed behind red glass. The
interior smelled of wax, old wood, and the specific kind of fatigue that
collects in places where the poor come to ask God for the sort of help that
the rich pay lawyers to provide.

Fr. Pawel met them in shirtsleeves with a black vest over them and a face
that had not wasted much time on vanity. He was a working priest, not a
cathedral ornament. Thick hands. Sallow skin. Eyes that had learned long
ago that every confession began as partial truth and improved only under
pressure.

Aiden handled the opening because he had the kind of voice that made even
defensive men imagine, briefly, that cooperation had been their own idea.
Nell helped when help was needed. Sean stood where Fr. Pawel could see the
scar on his face and decide for himself what sort of company had come
calling. Mikel took in the room the way he took in everything: the altar,
the narrow windows, the small cough from somewhere in back, the tremor in
the priest's fingers when the word asylum entered the conversation.

It was that word that mattered.

Possession, Aiden said. Or something mistaken for possession. Had the
Church heard anything?

At first Fr. Pawel did what men in his position always do when deciding
whether the truth will improve their day. He became precise.

"The Church hears many things," he said. "Few of them improve in the
retelling."

Nell leaned in just enough to suggest that they were beyond retelling.

"A man is dead," she said. "A lot of people are acting wrong. If you've
seen the edge of it, we need that edge."

Fr. Pawel looked at her, then at Aiden, then at Sean, and perhaps decided
that whatever game Arkham was playing, it had already moved past the stage
where silence felt responsible.

He told them there had been a case at the asylum.

Not recent. Not old enough to forget.

The staff had called for him because one of the patients — or perhaps an
inmate, depending on which part of the institution a man had offended — was
said to be speaking with another voice, knowing what he should not know,
staring at the walls as if the walls were crowded. The orderlies were
frightened. One of the nurses was more frightened than the rest, which, to
Fr. Pawel's mind, was sometimes evidence all by itself.

He went. He prayed. He observed.

And in the end, he said, it was not possession.

"Then what was it?" Nell asked.

Fr. Pawel's mouth flattened.

"Suffering," he said. "Fear. A mind in ruins. Perhaps wickedness. Perhaps
some influence. But not the thing people say when they want a story big
enough to excuse what they do not understand."

Aiden watched him closely. "You remember it too clearly for a simple
mistake."

That gave the priest pause, and in the pause they all saw that the answer
to that was yes.

"I remember it," Fr. Pawel said, "because it was not clean."

Not demonic, then. Not holy. Something else. Something with the outline of
religion but none of the comfort. He would not say more, either because he
did not know more or because the rest of it still sat somewhere in him like
a stone.

They left with no absolution, no exorcism, and one useful thing besides a
priest's unease: the asylum had joined the map of the case.

Outside, the wind had picked up. A church bell struck the hour somewhere
farther downhill. Arkham seemed, if anything, even less inclined toward
peace than before.


                              II.
                      THE MEN WITH PURPOSE


The street outside St. Stanislaus gave them the sort of brief, false calm
that often arrives just before a city decides to embarrass everyone in it.

They had a church behind them, the library still waiting across town, and
the case spread out ahead in too many directions at once. Nell was already
turning Fr. Pawel's words into possible lines of inquiry. Asylum records.
Nurses. Orderlies. Who had called the priest in the first place. Aiden was
thinking in cleaner lines than that: what moved first, where to apply
pressure, which door would open before midnight and which would not. Sean
was smoking and thinking about the King in Shadow's name in the only terms
that mattered to him — money, territory, fear, and whether the man who wore
that title was smart enough to deserve it. Mikel was watching the street.

So he saw them, or rather he saw Aiden notice them and then looked where
Aiden looked, which in practice amounted to much the same thing.

Five men. Maybe six. Ragged coats. Bad shoes. Hats pulled low. The sort of
men Arkham trained itself not to see at all unless one of them started
shouting or dying. But they were not drifting. They were not begging, and
they were not wandering toward warmth. They moved in a loose file with a
shared direction and a shared pace, like laborers late for a shift or
soldiers ordered not to break formation.

That was what made them wrong.

There is a difference between poverty and purpose. Aiden knew it. Nell knew
it two seconds after he did. Sean, slower to the exact shape of it but not
to the smell of trouble, saw enough in the line of their movement to stop
smoking without seeming to notice he had done so.

"You see that?" Aiden said quietly.

"I do," Nell said.

Sean flicked ash into the gutter and said, "They're going somewhere."

Yes. And going with intent.

They followed at a distance that would have looked accidental to anyone not
already thinking like a detective or a criminal, which by now described the
group well enough. The men moved south and west through the city without
speaking. Past a corner store. Past a newspaper boy whose call went thin
when none of them turned to look. Past a parked truck with nobody in it and
crates under a tarp. Arkham traffic parted around them unconsciously, the
way crowds part around sleepwalkers or funeral processions.

Nell wrote nothing down. There are times when a reporter knows that taking
out the notebook will change the temperature of a moment, and this was one
of them.

Mikel tracked by rhythm more than meaning. The men ahead moved too evenly.
Too little hesitation. Too little self-correction. Even drunks have
wobble. Even hungry men have argument in their shoulders. These men moved
as if a single idea had been issued to all of them and none had anything
personal left to add.

The city tightened around them as they went. The university buildings gave
way to the business streets nearer College and Garrison. The Hotel
Miskatonic rose into view ahead across from the Orne Library, brick and
respectability and concealed rot, every inch of it suddenly looking more
important than it had an hour earlier.

Sean saw the hotel and swore softly under his breath.

He knew that building now. Knew what it meant. Knew who lived in it.

"No," he said, almost to himself.

But the men were already crossing the street.

They did not hesitate at the entrance. Did not stop to look up. Did not do
any of the little human things that accompany fear when fear is still
permitted to exist. They went into the Hotel Miskatonic in a line, and for
one stretched second the world held still around the shape of that choice.

Then the first shot came from inside.


                              III.
                           THE HOTEL


Gunfire in a city like Arkham has a different quality than gunfire in a
place that expects it.

In Chicago, perhaps, or New York, the sound might have been folded at once
into the larger noise of the place and identified for what it was with
resigned efficiency. In Arkham it arrived first as confusion. A backfire. A
crate dropped on wood. Somebody slamming a door hard enough to make the
glass answer.

Then came the second shot.

Then the burst after that, and the street understood.

The lobby windows of the Hotel Miskatonic spat light and shattered. People
on the sidewalk dropped without dignity and crawled where they could. A
woman screamed from somewhere too close. Somebody yelled for the police in
the tone of a man who had only just remembered that the police existed. The
sound rolled up the hotel stairwell and out through broken glass and across
College Street hard enough to make the very stones of the library seem to
listen.

Sean did not move toward it. Sean moved sideways, instinctively, for angle
and cover, because whatever else this was, it was organized violence and he
had lived too close to organized violence not to recognize the pattern.
Multiple guns. Controlled pace. Target-rich interior. Not a panic. A hit.

Only not the kind of hit he expected.

These were not Potrello's polished men or O'Bannion's own hard faces turned
against him. These were wrecks. Street men. The kind you stepped over on a
cold morning if you were in a hurry and not trying very hard to be decent.
Yet inside the hotel those same men were firing with ghastly commitment,
driving through the building as if they had been wound for one purpose and
one purpose only.

Nell saw the first Irishman go down in the lobby and knew, with the clean
horror that only reporters and police ever feel, that this was the sort of
story that would eclipse every other story in town by nightfall. A hotel in
the university district. A daylight massacre. Gangsters. Civilians. Broken
glass in the street. Wallace Baines would lose his mind over the copy. That
thought came and went, replaced by the more immediate one that being near a
story and surviving a story were not identical occupations.

Aiden's eyes moved constantly. Entrances. Witnesses. Who was running. Who
wasn't. He saw a bellhop bolt from the side door with blood on one sleeve
that was not his own. He saw a clerk flatten himself behind an automobile.
He saw two muzzle flashes from an upper window where there should not have
been any gunmen yet at all.

Mikel knew the sound and not the language of it. He knew enough to count
direction. Some from below. Some from higher up. Men were shouting in
English and not all of the voices sounded like the men who had entered.

It went on too long.

That was what stayed with all of them later. Not merely that it happened,
but that it kept happening past the point where common sense wanted the
world to intervene and stop it.

Then, as suddenly as a door shutting, it ended.

Smoke drifted from the blown lobby windows. A car horn somewhere nearby had
started and forgotten how to stop. The silence that followed was not true
silence but the stunned, ringing counterfeit of it that arrives when human
ears have been given more violence than they can immediately sort.

Heads lifted. People breathed.

Then somebody pointed upward.

Four men stood on the edge of the penthouse balcony.

Not Irish muscle. Not hotel guests. The same type as the shooters who had
gone in — ragged, underfed, anonymous. One of them had blood down the front
of his coat. Another seemed almost calm. They stood shoulder to shoulder
against the lowering sky, and for one impossible second they looked less
like men deciding than like men waiting for a signal only they could hear.

No signal came that anyone else could see.

They stepped out together anyway.

The crowd made the sound crowds make when death becomes public and vertical.
Someone cried out the name of God. One body struck an awning and rolled.
Another hit the pavement cleanly and did not move again. The third twisted
in the air as if trying belatedly to change its mind. The fourth landed bad
and lived just long enough to become useful.

Sean was nearest. Aiden only a step behind him. Nell forced herself closer
than she wanted because words spoken by dying men were sometimes the only
honest words a city got all week.

The man's chest worked twice. Blood bubbled at the corner of his mouth. His
eyes did not look like a sane man's eyes. They looked wide and obedient,
like a horse run past exhaustion.

He said four words and spent the last of his life on the third.

"The King in Shadow..."

Then he was empty.

Nell felt the name hit her a second time, harder now because it had arrived
with broken bones and brick dust and a dead man's blood on the street. June
had not been passing gossip. June had been standing at the edge of a very
large machine and describing the noise it made.

Sean looked up at the hotel again, at the broken windows, at the penthouse,
and the facts lined up in him with the grim efficiency of old instinct.

"Danny O'Bannion," he said.

Nobody answered at first.

"That was O'Bannion's building," Sean said. "Whoever did this, they hit
him."

Ashburn and his candles and his dress-up cult suddenly looked, not
irrelevant, but very far from sufficient.


                              IV.
                        THE ROOM ABOVE


It was Mikel who went in.

Perhaps because he was quickest on his feet. Perhaps because his first
instinct in chaos was always practical. Perhaps because the others were
still pinned by shock to the street and Mikel, who had crossed an ocean and
fought rivers and language both, had less faith than the rest in waiting
for sense to appear if you simply stood still long enough.

He slipped through the broken entrance and into the Hotel Miskatonic while
the survivors were still deciding whether the danger had ended or merely
changed floors.

The lobby smelled of powder smoke, plaster, hot metal, and a fresh arterial
wetness that no amount of expensive wood paneling could improve. Bodies lay
where they had fallen — one half behind the registration desk, one facedown
by the stairs, another draped over the banister in an attitude so awkward
that for an instant it almost looked theatrical. It was not theatrical.
Death had a way of becoming sincere the moment you got close enough.

Mikel moved carefully. Up one flight, then another. The sounds below had
thinned to groans, breaking glass, someone outside shouting for an
ambulance as if ambulances could undo anything that had happened here.

The higher he climbed, the less the scene looked like a firefight.

There were signs of violence, yes. Doors flung wide. Splintered wood. A
smear of blood along the wall where a wounded man had caught himself before
sliding down out of sight. But the disorder did not have the shape of the
lobby's public slaughter. It felt narrower. More deliberate. Personal.

At the penthouse level he found the door ajar.

He put one hand to it, pushed, and entered.

The room beyond had once been handsome in the style of men who mistake
expense for taste. Heavy drapes. Good liquor. A view over the street and
campus beyond. Rugs thick enough to imply that other people cleaned them.
Now the place had been turned into a slaughter room.

Danny O'Bannion was on the wall.

Not leaning against it. Not collapsed at its base. On it.

Pinned there in the blasphemous parody of a specimen mounted for display,
his body held upright by sheer violence. Something long and hard had driven
through him and into the plaster behind. His coat hung open. His shirt was
dark with blood. And his chest — his chest had been worked over with a
dagger again and again and again, the repeated thrusts so close together
that even to a man with no English for the scene the meaning was clear:
rage, message, certainty.

This had not happened by accident in the confusion downstairs. The men who
shot up the hotel had provided noise. This room had provided intention.

Mikel stepped nearer because he was built that way, because some people
retreated from the center of horror and some closed distance in order to
understand it. The dead gangster's face had lost the authority it once
possessed. All corpses do eventually. Power leaves faster than blood.

O'Bannion had been one of the hidden pillars of the case without most of
them even knowing it. Sean had sensed the shape. Nell had heard the name.
Now the pillar was dead and nailed to a wall by somebody who wanted Arkham
to know that old arrangements were over.

Mikel did not stay. He was no fool. Police would come. Surviving gunmen, if
there were any left, might still be somewhere in the building. And there
was little to be gained from being discovered alone in the penthouse with a
dead mob boss and no English sufficient to explain himself.

He came back down as quickly and quietly as he had gone up.

Outside, Sean read the answer on his face before Mikel found the words with
his hands and fragments.

Dead. Upstairs. Wall. Knife.

Sean went pale in the particular way men like Sean do — not with weakness
but with calculation arriving all at once. If O'Bannion was dead upstairs
before the police arrived, before the newspapers, before the city had even
decided what story to tell about the broken glass and bodies on the
sidewalk, then this was bigger than a gang score.

"We go," Aiden said.

For once nobody argued.

They left the Hotel Miskatonic behind them to the sirens already beginning
to gather at the edges of the district.


                              V.
                          THE TASTE OF IT


Night took the city in stages.

By the time they reached the docks again, Arkham had already begun the
process every city begins after violence: telling itself a smaller story
than the truth. People were saying Italians. People were saying O'Bannion.
People were saying turf, reprisal, score-settling. Those things had the
advantage of being terrible in ordinary ways. Ordinary terrors are always
preferred. You can sleep beside them if you are tired enough.

The dockside bar they chose was the sort of place where all the lamps had
been bought secondhand and all the wood had absorbed too many elbows to
believe in refinement anymore. Cheap whiskey. Wet wool. Men whose work had
become less regular than their thirst. It was close enough to the river to
smell it every time the door opened.

And tonight it had what they had been looking for.

The new liquor.

Not announced. Not advertised. Just present in the bottle the bartender set
down with the little proprietary satisfaction of a man who had finally
gotten hold of something other bars would soon envy him for.

Sean took the glass because of course he did.

Some men are built to test a thing by meeting it head-on. Some because they
are brave, some because they are reckless, and some because life has taught
them that hesitation is merely another way of volunteering to be lied to.
Sean Murphy had a little of all three.

He drank.

The liquor hit his tongue almost gently.

That was the first wrongness. Not harsh. Not raw. No chemical bite warning
the body that this was gutter poison wearing a decent suit. It went down
warm and smooth and with a fullness of taste that made ordinary whiskey
seem suddenly cheap and underambitious by comparison. There was sweetness
under the burn, and something darker under the sweetness, and then the
whole thing opened in his chest like a door.

For one brief, dangerous moment, the world improved.

His shoulder mattered less. The edge came off the room. The air itself felt
more cooperative. All the strain of the day — the hotel, the blood, the
dead men on the pavement, the priest, the name, the city pressing in from
every direction — receded by half a step, enough to make him understand at
once why men would come back for another drink and then another after that.

He did not look drunk. He looked convinced.

Nell watched the change in him and disliked it instantly. Not because Sean
became softer. He did not. Because for the first time since she had begun
pulling at the cursed-liquor thread, she saw the real shape of the trap.
Bad liquor was easy to denounce. Men died of bad liquor every year. The law
made speeches. Reporters wrote columns. Widows buried husbands. It was a
recognizable civic sin.

This was worse.

This was a product people would ask for.

Aiden tested the room instead of the bottle. Who drank. Who watched.
Whether the bartender was proud or merely relieved. Whether the two men who
came in through the back ten minutes later were customers or carriers.

Carriers, as it happened.

They brought cases.

Wooden, straw-packed, moved with the practiced lack of drama of men
delivering ordinary commerce. Which was the point. Arkham's doom had not
come wrapped in horns and chanting. It had come in cases through the back
door of a riverside bar while the town was still talking about a hotel
shooting.

Nell tried twice to get more from the bartender once the new cases appeared.
Destination, source, owner, anything. But success at the Gilded Swan had
not guaranteed success here. The man had his mood and his loyalties and his
limit, and she found the limit first.

He laughed instead.

"You folks worry too much," he said, one hand resting affectionately on the
fresh shipment as though it were a good dog or a newborn child. "This is
the stuff now."

Sean looked at the bottle again.

The bartender spread his hands to include the room, the river, the district,
the whole wet tired city beyond the walls.

"Whole town'll be drinking it soon."

That was the line that closed the chapter on them.

Outside, somewhere upriver, a boat horn sounded through the fog. Inside,
the fresh cases waited under lamplight like promise or threat depending on
how honest a man was prepared to be.

And in Sean Murphy's mouth, long after the glass was empty, the taste
lingered.

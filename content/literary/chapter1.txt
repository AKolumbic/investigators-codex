MADNESS ON THE MISKATONIC
A Literary Account of Events

CHAPTER ONE — THE GATHERING


                              I.
                   THE WOMAN WHO ASKED QUESTIONS


It had been raining for three days. Not the honest rain of summer storms but
the grey, persistent drizzle of a New England May — the kind that seeped into
wool and brick and bone and stayed there, cold and patient, as if the sky
itself had decided that Arkham did not deserve to dry.

The Arkham Gazette newsroom occupied the second floor of a narrow building on
the corner of West Hyde and Jenkin Streets, and on this particular morning it
sounded like what it was: a
factory for the manufacture of small truths. Typewriters clattered in
overlapping rhythms, a mechanical rainfall that echoed the real thing streaking
down the tall windows. Cigarette smoke hung in layers beneath the ceiling
lamps, drifting in slow, flat currents that never quite reached the floor.
Telephones rang and were answered and rang again. The air smelled of ink and
tobacco and damp wool and the coffee that had been on the hot plate since six
o'clock and had long since passed from beverage into punishment.

Eleanor Carroway — Nell to her editor, Miss Carroway to everyone else — sat
at her desk in the back corner, near the archive shelves, and did not hear any
of it. She had her scrapbook open. The scrapbook was a battered thing, fat
with clippings and notes, held together by a rubber band and the particular
faith of a woman who believed that if you collected enough fragments of the
truth, the truth itself would eventually appear. She had been keeping it for
months now — pasting in stories from a dozen papers across New England, drawing
red pencil circles around names and dates, connecting incidents that no one else
had thought to connect.

A mill worker in Innsmouth who had bitten off his own tongue in the middle of
a shift, screaming about something in the water. A fisherman's wife in
Kingsport found wandering the beach at dawn, her fingernails torn to the quick,
unable to say what she had been digging for. A traveling salesman in Dunwich who
had driven his automobile off the road at noon on a clear day, claiming
afterward that the road had moved. A professor's wife in Boston discovered in
her garden at three in the morning, on her knees in the mud, weeping, her hands
raw and bleeding, unable to explain what had compelled her to dig.

Individually, each was a local tragedy. A breakdown. Bad nerves. Bad luck.
The kind of thing that happens when the world comes apart at the seams and
people's minds come apart with it.

Taken together, they were a pattern.

Nell Carroway had never met a pattern she could leave alone.

A note landed on her desk. It arrived the way notes always arrived from
Wallace Baines — dropped from a height of approximately six inches, with the
casual authority of a man delivering a verdict he had already moved past.
Baines was her editor, a broad man in shirtsleeves with a face like a catcher's
mitt and a voice that could carry across the newsroom without rising above
conversational volume. His gruffness concealed a protective instinct he would
die before admitting to. He called Nell's obsession with connections her
"nose." She called it the reason she couldn't sleep.

The note was a wire report. Another disturbance at a Northside drinking
establishment. A laborer named Gerald Fiske had attacked two men with a broken
bottle, raving about shapes in the walls. Third such incident this month.

Nell looked up.

"Wallace."

Baines was already turning away. He stopped.

"Third one," she said. She flipped the scrapbook back two pages, three pages.
"All Northside. All men with no prior history of violence. All raving about
things that aren't there." She tapped a clipping. "A woman on Parsonage Street
tried to claw her own eyes out in March. A dockworker walked into the river in
April babbling in a language his wife didn't recognize. None of these made more
than a paragraph in the paper. There's a pattern here, Wallace. Someone is —"

"You're seeing connections where there's just bad hooch and bad luck, Nell."
Baines said it the way a man closes a door — firmly, but without slamming it.
"Write up the Fiske piece. Eight inches, no more."

He did not tell her not to investigate. He knew better than to try.

Among her clippings, she found a name she had circled weeks ago: Prof. Gordon
Fairchild, Dept. of Psychology, Miskatonic University. Quoted twice as an
expert on mass hysteria and the psychology of superstition. A skeptic. A
rationalist. The kind of man who explained things. She had meant to interview
him about the madness cases. She had never gotten around to it.

Then Vic Valentine's letter had arrived at the Gazette offices, addressed to
her personally, and the coincidence stopped being a coincidence and became a
summons.

Today felt like the day.

Nell pulled on her coat, tucked the scrapbook into her bag, and went to find
out what had happened to Gerald Fiske.


                              *


The Speakeasy on North Garrison Street was an O'Bannion joint. Everyone in Arkham knew
it; nobody in Arkham said it. The Irish mob ran their liquor through the place
with the quiet efficiency of a municipal water system, and the Arkham Police
Department looked the other way with a commitment that bordered on the
spiritual. It was not the kind of establishment that welcomed reporters, and it
was not, at this hour, open for business.

Nell stood on the sidewalk in the rain and knocked.

Nothing happened. She knocked again.

The door opened three inches. A chain caught it. Through the gap, she could
see a sliver of Mikey Catchpole — one eye, half a mouth, the edge of a jaw
that had learned early not to give anything away. He was a wiry man, early
forties, with the particular stillness of a person who understood that in
Arkham, in 1930, a bartender in an O'Bannion establishment who gave things away
did not remain a bartender for long.

"We're closed."

"I know you're closed, Mikey." Nell's collar was up but the rain had found
its way in anyway, running in cold lines down the back of her neck. She did not
step forward. She did not reach for the door. She stood where she was and
spoke through the three-inch gap with the practiced ease of a woman who had
conducted half her career's best interviews on the wrong side of a threshold.
"The Fiske thing. You were here that night?"

"Lot of people were here that night."

"I'm not asking about a lot of people." Her voice dropped — not to a whisper,
but to something more intimate. Conspiratorial. The voice of a woman sharing a
secret rather than extracting one. "A man named Fiske walked into this
establishment, sat down, poured something into his drink, and then tore his own
face open. Three column inches in the Advertiser. Buried on page six. I'm not
here to put your name in a paper, Mikey. I'm here because the next time it
happens — and there will be a next time — I'd like to understand why."

The rain fell. The door stayed open three inches. Through the gap, Catchpole's
eye moved — to the street behind her, to the wet sidewalk, back to Nell's face.
Something shifted in the geometry of the visible half of his expression. Not
trust. Trust was not a commodity traded freely in Northside Arkham. But the
resistance went out of his voice the way air goes out of a tire — slowly, and
with a faint sound of defeat.

"He hadn't been here long." Catchpole's voice came through the crack low and
flat, as if the smaller the opening, the quieter the confession needed to be.
"Came in alone. I'd seen him around the docks before — not a regular, not in
here. He sat down, ordered a whiskey. Then he pulled out this flask." A pause.
"His own flask. Poured something from it into the glass. That's when things
went sideways."

Nell's pen moved across her notepad in the shorthand she had developed over
ten years of interviews with people who did not want to be interviewed. The
rain blurred the ink almost as fast as she wrote it. "So the liquor that set
him off — it wasn't yours."

"It wasn't ours." Through the gap, Catchpole's jaw tightened. "Whatever was
in that flask, he brought it with him. From the docks, if I had to guess. The
man reeked of the waterfront."

She filed this away with the quiet precision of a librarian shelving a book
she knew she would need again. The spiked product was not coming through
O'Bannion's supply chain. The Speakeasy's stock was clean — or as clean as
illegal liquor ever was. Whatever had turned Fiske into a screaming, clawing
wreck had come from somewhere else. A separate channel. A separate supplier.
And that channel ran through the docks.

Behind her, the sound of an automobile engine cut through the rain. Headlights
swept across the wet street, across Nell's back, across the three-inch gap in
the door. Catchpole's eye tracked the light. The openness — such as it was —
snapped shut like a ledger at the end of the day.

The door moved.

"We're done," he said.

"Mikey —"

The door closed. The chain rattled. The lock turned. Nell stood alone on the
sidewalk in the rain, her notepad darkening in her hand, the ink already
beginning to run.

She did not turn around to look at the car. A black sedan, idling at the
curb, its occupants invisible behind rain-streaked glass. It might have been
nobody. It might have been somebody. In Arkham, in the rain, in 1930, the
distinction was not always clear.

Nell turned up her collar and started walking south toward the Garrison
Street Bridge.

The bridge was old iron and older stone, spanning the Miskatonic where the
river bent hard against the Northside bank. In better weather it was merely
ugly. In the rain it was a grey scar over brown water, and the wind came up
the channel and cut through her coat as if the coat were not there. She walked
with her head down, her shoes loud on the wet planking, and she was halfway
across when something made her stop.

Below, on the riverbank. Movement.

She leaned against the railing and looked down. The rain and the failing
light made it difficult — the bank was a confusion of mud and scrub and the
dark shapes of rocks — but there were figures down there. Two of them, maybe
three. Moving along the waterline in the careful, deliberate way of men who
did not want to be seen. One of them carried something — a lantern, maybe, or
a torch, though it was not lit. They moved upriver toward the Garrison Street
pilings and then the bend of the bank took them out of sight.

It might have been fishermen. It might have been dockworkers pulling a late
shift. There were a hundred innocent explanations for men on a riverbank in
the rain.

Nell watched the place where they had disappeared for a long moment. Then she
pulled the scrapbook tighter against her body to keep the rain off it and
continued south toward Miskatonic University, where a professor named Fairchild
kept an office in Warren Hall, and where — if Vic Valentine's letter was to be
believed — the answers to her pattern might finally be waiting.


                              II.
                    THE MAN WHO RAN RUM


Sean Murphy came into Arkham the way trouble usually does — from the
direction nobody was watching.

He drove his prized Studebaker Whiskey Six down the Aylesbury Pike with both hands on
the wheel and his jaw set against the road. The pike was bad in good weather
and murderous in rain — a dirt track masquerading as a county road, rutted and
washed-out, winding through hill country that seemed to lean in from both sides
as if the land itself were trying to close the path behind him. Twice he had
muscled the car through standing water that came up over the running boards.
Once, rounding a bend above a nameless creek, the back end had broken loose on
the mud and he had corrected it without thinking, the way a man corrects a
thing he has corrected a thousand times before.

The scar on his face itched in the damp. It always did.

The Glasgow smile. Two pale ridges of scar tissue running from the corners
of his mouth toward his ears, pulling the skin taut, giving him a permanent
expression that was not quite a grin and not quite a grimace but something in
between that made strangers look away and acquaintances choose their words with
care. He had received it in Detroit, in a room he did not talk about, from men
whose names he had never learned. The Purple Gang had been making a point about
territorial boundaries. The point had been received. Sean Murphy had left
Detroit and had not gone back.

He was thirty-two years old and he had been running liquor since he was old
enough to drive. Whiskey out of Canada through the Detroit River crossings,
until a few boys from the Purple Gang carved that particular smile into his face and ran him out of town. Then south to New Orleans, where he had reinvented himself running
Caribbean rum through the port and moonshine out of the bayou, navigating the
swamp roads in the dark with the kind of easy competence that came from having
no better options and a constitutional inability to do honest work.

He had seen things in those swamps. Things he could not explain and did not
try to. Lights that moved against the current. Sounds that came from places
where nothing lived. Shapes in the water that were not alligators and were not
logs and were not anything that had a name in any language Sean spoke. He had
reported these sightings to his great-uncle Sheamus — the old man was a
naturalist professor at Miskatonic University by trade and a cryptid hunter by
obsession, the kind of whiskey-soaked academic who kept specimen jars on every
surface and maintained a filing cabinet of newspaper clippings about unexplained
sightings that his colleagues in the zoology department pretended not to know
about.

It was Sheamus who had called him here.

The outskirts of Arkham materialized through the rain like a photograph
developing in solution. Narrow streets running down to the river. Gambrel roofs
gone dark with wet. The spires of churches and the chimneys of the university
rising above a treeline of bare oaks, and beneath them the cramped, suspicious
geometry of a New England town that had been old when the Republic was young.

Sean drove through and did not see what there was to see. The warehouses
along the waterfront that were too busy for the hour. The trucks that moved
without running lights. The subtle infrastructure of a mature bootlegging
operation mapped across the town like a second nervous system — the loading
docks, the alley routes, the establishments with back doors that opened onto
roads that led to roads that led to nowhere the police cared to follow. He
would have recognized it, had he been looking. He had grown up inside exactly
this kind of machinery. He knew its rhythms the way a clockmaker knows gears.

But he was not looking. He was driving, and the rain was heavy, and his mind
was on his uncle's voice over the telephone — the urgency in it, the sobriety,
the things it had not said.


                              *


Sheamus Murphy's apartment was on the third floor of a narrow building in
Northside, up a stairwell that smelled of mildew and boiled cabbage and the
particular exhaustion of a rooming house in the Depression — a building full of
people who had stopped expecting things to get better and were now simply
managing the rate of decline. Sean took the stairs two at a time and knocked.

The door opened immediately. Sheamus had been waiting.

He was older than Sean remembered — though Sean's memories of the old man
were patchwork things, assembled from childhood visits and telephone calls and
the occasional letter written in handwriting that suggested the author had
been drinking steadily since breakfast. He was tall and going to bone, with a
shock of white hair that stood up as if permanently startled, and eyes that
were too bright for the rest of his face. The apartment behind him was exactly
what Sean would have expected: cramped, chaotic, every surface buried under
books and specimen jars and old maps and newspaper clippings about things that
respectable scientists did not discuss in faculty meetings. A half-empty bottle
of Jameson sat on the kitchen table. Two more empties stood under the sink.
The place smelled like pipe tobacco, formaldehyde, and whiskey that had been
sweated out overnight.

But Sheamus himself was sober. At this hour. Sean noted this the way a
soldier notes a change in the wind.

"Right, yer here. Good. Sit down." Sheamus waved him toward a chair, then
lunged to remove a stack of journals from it. "No — don't sit there, that's —
never mind. Listen."

Sean sat. He did not remove his coat. The apartment was cold and the
radiator was doing the kind of nothing that suggested it had been doing nothing
for some time.

"There's a fella," Sheamus said. He did not sit down. He paced — three steps
to the window, three steps back, the restless circuit of a man whose thoughts
were moving faster than his body could accommodate. "A colleague of mine.
Fairchild. Gordon Fairchild, over in the psychology department. Grand skeptic,
that one — wouldn't believe in ghosts if one sat on his face. But he's a good
man, Sean. A serious man. And something's gone wrong with him."

"Wrong how?"

Sheamus reached for the Jameson. Stopped himself. Put his hand flat on the
table instead.

"Wrong in a way I can't quite put me finger on. Distracted. Exhausted.
Canceling lectures. He looks like a man who hasn't slept in a month, and when
you ask him about it he smiles and says he's fine, and the smile doesn't reach
anywhere near his eyes." Sheamus turned from the window. "I'd have written it
off — sure, it's 1930, everyone's wrecked — but then there's Vic."

"Vic."

"Valentine. Vic Valentine. A detective, out of Boston. Good man. Good
drinking buddy, which is the higher compliment." A ghost of a smile. "He came
to Arkham a few weeks back — said he had a case, something to do with strange
goings-on at the university. Fairchild brought him in. The two of them were
working together, looking into some trouble — cult business, fellas in robes,
symbols scratched on walls, the whole circus. I helped where I could. Vic would
come round here in the evenings and we'd have a jar and he'd tell me what he'd
found, and I'd tell him what I knew about the local color, and it was grand."

Sheamus stopped pacing. He stood in the middle of the room, his hands at his
sides, and when he spoke again his voice was different. Quieter. The brogue
thinned out the way it always did when the old man was being serious — as if
the accent were a costume he wore for everyday life and took off for the things
that mattered.

"He stopped coming around about a few days ago. No word, no note, nothing.
That's not right. That's not like him at all. He was working with Fairchild,
and Fairchild says he doesn't know where Vic's gone, and I believe him because
the man looks bloody terrified, Sean, and I don't think he's terrified of me
asking questions. I think he's terrified of something else entirely."

"And you called me."

"I called you." Sheamus met his eyes. "Because you've seen things, Sean. The
things you told me about, down in the bayou — the lights, the shapes in the
water, the sounds that come from places where nothing should be living. I
didn't put them in the clippings file as curiosities. I put them in because I
believe you. Because I've spent thirty years at this university pretending to be
a respectable naturalist while collecting evidence of things that are not
natural at all, and what you saw in Louisiana is part of a pattern that goes
back centuries, and whatever is happening to Fairchild and whatever happened to
Vic — I think it's part of the same bloody thing."

He paused. His hand drifted toward the Jameson again, and this time he did
not stop it. He poured two fingers and did not drink them. He just held the
glass and looked at it.

"And because you can get heavy, if it comes to that. Which I think it might."

Sean said nothing for a long moment. The rain hit the windows. The radiator
ticked. Somewhere below them in the building, a door slammed and a woman's
voice rose in a language Sean didn't recognize and subsided again into the
general silence of a place where people had learned to keep their misery at a
manageable volume.

"Where do I find Fairchild?"

"Warren Hall. Room 214. Psychology department." Sheamus set the glass down,
untouched. "And for the love of God, Sean. Be careful. I mean it. I'm not
joking with you."

Sean stood. He put a hand on the old man's shoulder — briefly, the way
Murphys touched, which was rarely and never for long — and went back out into
the rain.

He parked the Studebaker on a side street near the campus and walked to
Warren Hall with his collar up and his hands in his pockets. The Colt .38
Super rode under his coat the way it always did — a familiar weight against
his ribs, warm from his body, patient. Uncle Sheamus had called him because
Sean could get heavy. The gun was how.


                              III.
                      THE MAN WHO DRIFTED


The Boston & Maine pulled into Arkham station at quarter past four on a
Wednesday afternoon, and Bill O'Job stepped off the train into rain that hit
the platform like handfuls of gravel.

He had nine dollars in his pocket. He had a canvas bag over one shoulder that
contained everything he owned. He had a letter from Vic Valentine folded into
the breast pocket of a coat that was not warm enough for Massachusetts in May.
The letter said: I need help. Come to Arkham. I'll explain when you get here.
That was all. No details. No explanation. Just a name and an address and the
implicit understanding, between men who had shared a particular kind of
experience, that when one of them called, the other came.

Bill was thirty-five years old and he had nothing. Not nothing in the way
that people say nothing when they mean not enough — nothing in the way that
means the number in your pocket is the number that stands between you and the
street. He had been nothing for a while now. A cowboy out of Amarillo, Texas,
trained in the work of open country — breaking horses, reading ground, sleeping
under skies so wide they made a man feel both free and crushingly small — and
then the war had come and he had gone to France and learned a different set of
skills, and then the war had ended and the skills he had learned were the kind
that did not translate to peacetime, and the country he had come home to was
not the country he had left.

The Depression had finished what the war started. There was no work. There
was no work anywhere, but there was especially no work for a man whose
qualifications were that he could track a man across broken ground, throw a
punch that ended arguments, and shoot the wings off a fly at forty yards. These
were valuable skills in certain contexts. In 1930, the contexts had dried up.

He had met Vic Valentine in the Argonne Forest in 1918. He did not talk
about the Argonne. Most men who had been there did not. But Vic had been steady
when steadiness mattered — not brave in the way that got men killed, but solid
in the way that kept them alive — and afterward, in a field hospital outside
Verdun, waiting to learn which of their friends had died, Vic had shared a
cigarette with Bill and said nothing at all for twenty minutes. That silence
had meant more than anything anyone had said to him in months.

When Vic's letter came, Bill had not hesitated. He had counted his money,
bought a train ticket, and left behind a rooming house in Boston where the
landlord had been making pointed observations about the rent.

The station platform told him nothing. Bill was a tracker by instinct and
training — a man who read the world through its signs the way a scholar reads
a page — but wet cobblestones gave up no secrets, and the Arkham B&M station
was just a small brick building in the rain.

He tried the porter. A thin young man behind a window, hunched against the
cold in a uniform that was too large for him.

"I'm looking for Miskatonic University."

"Straight down Church Street. Can't miss it."

Bill tried the friendly approach — a smile, an open posture, a tone that
suggested two men sharing a conversation rather than one man extracting
information from another.

"I'm also looking for a man who might have —"

"Straight down Church Street."

The friendliness had not worked. Bill was not, if he was honest with himself,
a man built for friendliness. He was built for other things. He leaned forward.
He was a big man — not tall, but broad across the shoulders and heavy in the
hands, with the kind of physical density that made furniture seem fragile — and
when he leaned into a conversation, the conversation noticed.

The porter's eyes came up and stayed up.

"Now," Bill said quietly. "I appreciate the directions. But I'm going to ask
you one more question, and I'd be grateful if you took a moment to think about
your answer. Miskatonic University. Which building is the psychology
department?"

The porter swallowed. "Warren Hall, sir. Room 214. Professor Fairchild." He
paused. "There's been a few people asking about him lately."

"Obliged."

Bill shouldered his bag and walked out into the rain. Behind him, at the far
end of the platform, a man sat alone on a bench. His collar was up and his
head was down, and he was rocking gently — forward and back, forward and back —
with the slow, mindless rhythm of a metronome. His lips were moving around
words that made no sound. His hands, resting on his knees, trembled with a
fine, constant vibration, like the hands of a man holding something heavy that
only he could see. His eyes, if anyone had looked into them, were wrong. The
pupils blown wide open despite the grey daylight, fixed on a point in the
middle distance where there was nothing at all.

Bill O'Job walked right past him.

He would think about that later. He would lie in a narrow bed in a town that
was not his own and think about the man on the bench and wonder what he had
looked like, what he had been saying, whether the trembling had been drink or
cold or something else entirely. He would wonder what he might have learned if
he had stopped. If he had looked.

But he did not stop. He did not look. He walked out into the rain and
followed Church Street south toward the university, nine dollars in his pocket
and the particular doomed luck of a man for whom nothing ever quite went right.


                              IV.
                      THE MAN WHO COULDN'T SPEAK


The Miskatonic River in May ran fast and brown and mean.

It was a New England river in flood — swollen with three weeks of rain, thick
with mud and timber and the accumulated debris of a dozen tributary streams,
running between its banks with the heavy, purposeful momentum of something that
had somewhere to be and did not care what it carried along the way. The current
moved at a pace that discouraged swimming and complicated navigation and had
drowned, according to local memory, at least two men in the last decade.

The fishing vessel that came upriver from the coast handled it the way a
horse handles a rider it does not respect.

Mikel Itsaso stood at the tiller and fought. He was a compact man of
thirty-seven, dark-haired, with the weathered hands and distant eyes of a
Basque sailor who had been arguing with the Atlantic since boyhood. Born in
Getaria, a fishing village on the coast of Gipuzkoa where the Cantabrian Sea
hammered the cliffs with a regularity that the villagers had long since
stopped noticing, Mikel had come to the new world the way many Basques came —
following the sea, following the fish, following the ancient, unbroken tradition
of a people who had been navigating by the stars when the rest of Europe was
still afraid of the horizon.

He had settled in Boston because the harbor was good and the work was steady
and because nobody in Boston asked a man where he was from if he could tie a
knot and haul a net. He worked with his hands — boats, buildings, anything
that needed fixing. He had a navigator's instinct for geography and a
pickpocket's gift for delicate work, and he spoke precisely six words of
English: yes, no, how much, thank you.

The letter from Vic had reached him through channels he did not fully
understand. Someone who knew someone who knew the detective. The letter itself
was in English, which Mikel could not read, but someone had translated the
essential message into French — which Mikel could not read either, but which
was close enough to Spanish that he got the bones of it.

Come to Arkham. The university. Danger.

That had been enough.

The docks at Arkham appeared through the rain and river mist, and Mikel
aimed for them, and the Miskatonic said no.

The first approach was wrong. The current caught the bow and shoved it wide,
and for a moment the boat swung broadside to the flow and the dock pilings
loomed close enough to tear the hull open. Mikel swore — in Euskara, the old
language, because Euskara was the only language in which he could swear with
the precision and creativity that the situation demanded — and hauled the
tiller over and fought the river with the stubborn fury of a man who had been
losing arguments with water his entire life and had not yet conceded a single
one.

The second approach was better. He read the current this time — its speed,
its angle, the way it curved against the dock — and set the boat against it
at a bias, letting the river push the hull sideways into the pilings with a
solid, controlled thump that was more competent than it sounded. He tied off
with hands that knew rope the way a musician's hands know keys and stepped
onto the dock and stood there in the rain, taking the measure of the town.

The dockhands watched him. One of them spoke — a question, probably, or a
greeting, or a demand for mooring fees. The words reached Mikel's ears and
disassembled there into meaningless sounds, the way a wave breaks into foam
against a seawall.

He said nothing.

Downstream, past the dock pilings where the bank curved toward a bridge,
there was some kind of commotion. People gathering at the waterline — a handful
at first, then more. A uniformed officer. The flash of a lantern against the
rain. Voices carried upriver but the words broke apart over the water before
they reached him, the way waves break into foam.

Mikel did not go to look. He had not come to Arkham to stand on a riverbank
with strangers. He had come to find a man.

He had a map. A hand-drawn thing, creased and water-spotted, with the
university circled in pencil and the river sketched in blue. He had studied it
on the voyage up the coast, memorizing the angles of the streets the way he
memorized coastlines — not as names on a page but as shapes in space, turns to
be navigated, landmarks to be read. He oriented himself against the river and
the compass in his head and began walking.

Mikel Itsaso found Miskatonic University without asking a single soul for
directions. He walked through Arkham as if it were a harbor he had visited
before, reading the town through its geometry rather than its language, and
arrived at Warren Hall with his clothes soaked through and his expression
perfectly, implacably blank.

He could not have told you, in any language, what it was about this town that
made the hair on his forearms stand up. He could not have described the quality
of the air — the way it seemed to thicken as he walked south from the river,
the faint pressure behind his eardrums, the sense of something vast and
patient and very close, the way the shadows between buildings seemed deeper
than the geometry of the structures could account for. He had no words for it.
He had very few words for anything.

But he felt it. The way a sailor feels a change in weather before the
instruments confirm it. The way an animal feels the tremor that precedes the
earthquake.

Something in Arkham was wrong.


                              V.
                          ROOM 214


Warren Hall was a brick building on the Miskatonic campus, three stories of
ivy and institutional authority, smelling of floor wax and pipe tobacco and the
particular mustiness of a building in which serious people had been thinking
serious thoughts for a very long time. Room 214 was on the second floor — a
wooden door with a brass nameplate that read PROF. GORDON FAIRCHILD,
DEPARTMENT OF PSYCHOLOGY. The door was open.

Sean Murphy arrived first.

Fairchild was behind his desk — a man somewhere past fifty, trim, with
silver-touched hair and the careful posture of a career academic, the kind of
man who had spent decades being observed by lecture halls and had learned to
control every visible surface. He wore a brown tweed suit. His spectacles were
round and slightly old-fashioned. The office was lined with books and smelled
of the pipe that rested in a ceramic tray at his elbow, and the overall effect
was of a man who had been assembled from a kit labeled PROFESSOR.

He looked up when Sean filled the doorway.

"Professor Fairchild? My uncle sent me. Sheamus Murphy."

Fairchild's eyes moved to Sean's face. They found the scar — the pale
ridges pulling at the corners of his mouth, the permanent not-quite-grin —
and lingered there for a fraction of a second, and then moved deliberately
away. It was a small thing. A flicker of attention, immediately suppressed.
But it told you something important about Gordon Fairchild: he noticed
everything and acknowledged only what he chose to. The scar might as well have
been a painting on the wall. Seen, catalogued, filed, never mentioned.

"Sheamus," Fairchild said. "Yes. Yes, of course. Please, come in."

Nell Carroway arrived next, still damp from the walk, her hat dripping, her
notepad already in her hand. She crossed the threshold asking questions the way
some people cross thresholds breathing — automatically, constitutionally, as an
expression of fundamental character.

Bill O'Job came in third, large and quiet. He took a chair against the wall
and sat with the patience of a man who had learned, in trenches and in
unemployment lines, that waiting was sometimes the most productive thing
you could do.

Mikel came in last.

He stood by the window and said nothing, because he could not. He surveyed
the room with the calm, appraising attention of a man on a foreign dock —
reading the space through its objects and its occupants rather than through
anything so unreliable as speech. After a moment, he took out a folding knife
and a pencil and began to sharpen the pencil with small, precise strokes. The
soft scraping sound was the only sound in the room.

Then he leaned toward Nell Carroway and attempted, with the exquisite
delicacy of a practiced hand, to slip the sharpened pencil into the breast
pocket of her blouse.

Nell's hand came up and cracked against his wrist like a trap snapping shut.

"Don't."

Mikel withdrew his hand without expression. The pencil remained in his grip.
He paused — not chastened, not apologetic, merely recalculating — and then
held up his other hand, palm out: peace. He motioned toward her notepad.

Nell watched him for a long, evaluating beat. Then she slid the notepad
across the table.

Mikel took the pencil he had just sharpened and wrote two words in large,
careful capitals:

                              VIK SOS

He pushed the notepad back. The room was very quiet. The rain tapped against
the window with the insistent patience of something that wanted to come in.
Four strangers looked at each other across a dead man's name — though they did
not yet know he was dead — and understood, without anyone saying it aloud, that
they were all here for the same reason.

Bill broke the silence. "I served with Vic. In France. He wrote to me three
weeks ago. Said he needed help."

"Same." Nell tapped her scrapbook. "Though I came for the pattern first.
Vic's letter confirmed what I was already following."

Sean said nothing about Sheamus. Nothing about the phone call, or the swamps,
or the things he had seen in the bayou that the old man had catalogued in his
notebooks with the meticulous attention of a man who did not expect to be
believed. Some things were family business.

Fairchild removed his spectacles and cleaned them with a handkerchief. It
was the gesture of a man buying time — small, deliberate, a way of putting a
moment of distance between the question and the answer. Four pairs of eyes
watched him do it, and three of them tried to read what was behind his
expression.

Bill watched the way he'd watched officers in the field — looking for the
tell, the flinch, the thing a man did with his hands when the words coming out
of his mouth weren't the whole story. Fairchild gave him nothing. The
professor's composure was a closed door, and Bill was not the kind of man who
found keyholes.

Sean watched the way he'd watched men across card tables and the hoods of
cars in midnight parking lots — looking for the angle, the play, the thing
that was being sold. Fairchild's surface was polished smooth as river glass.
If there was something underneath it, Sean couldn't find the edge.

Mikel, who could not understand the words and therefore had nothing to do but
watch the body, studied the angle of Fairchild's shoulders and the movement of
his hands and came away with nothing he could name and nothing he could be
sure of.

Nell watched.

She watched the way she had watched a thousand reluctant sources across a
thousand café tables and office desks and courthouse benches — not for what
they showed, but for what they were trying not to show. Fairchild's hands on
the spectacles, the slight tremor in the right thumb. His eyes when he put
them back on — where they went first, how long they stayed. The muscles around
his mouth, the set of his jaw, the particular quality of his stillness. A man
holding himself in place.

And she knew — with the certainty of a decade spent sitting across from
people who were trying very hard not to tell her things — that Gordon Fairchild
was afraid.

Not afraid of them. Afraid for someone.

He was worried about Vic Valentine. Genuinely, deeply worried. Whatever was
happening in Arkham, Gordon Fairchild's concern for the missing detective was
not performance.

Only Nell saw this. She kept it to herself.

Fairchild put his spectacles back on and folded his hands on the desk. When
he spoke, it was with the careful pacing of a man who had rehearsed a thing in
his head many times but had not yet said it aloud.

"Vic and I go back to Chicago. I met him while I was working at the university.
We kept in touch the way men do — Christmas cards, the occasional letter." 
He paused. "When I came to Miskatonic three years ago, I began hearing things.
Reports of strange behavior. Outbursts. People in town
acting in ways that defied clinical explanation. I am a psychologist and a
skeptic by training — I do not believe in the occult, I do not believe in
curses, and I have spent the better part of my career explaining the
irrational in rational terms. But the things I was hearing did not fit any
framework I could construct."

He looked at his hands.

"So I called Vic. I asked him to come to Arkham and help me investigate —
properly, professionally. To find the source of these disturbances and put a
rational explanation to them. To disprove the nonsense." A thin smile that did
not survive contact with the rest of his face. "He arrived several weeks ago.
We began looking into the local landscape together, and the trail led us to a
man named Elbridge Ashburn. Old Arkham money. Ashburn runs a kind of social
circle with — how shall I put this — occult interests. Robed figures, rituals,
symbols. The sort of thing that looks alarming until you examine it closely."

"Occult," Nell said. Flat.

"Vic examined it closely." Fairchild's voice was steady. His hands, resting
on the desk, were not. "He described the Ashburn group as — his exact words —
'amateur hour.' Not a serious threat. A rich man playing at dark rituals with
his society friends. Vic was convinced the real answer lay elsewhere." He
paused. "But he went back to look at Ashburn one more time, and he didn't come
back. That was days ago. I've been to his room at the Hotel Miskatonic — he's
paid through the end of May. His things are still there. His notes. But Vic
is gone."

The room absorbed this. The rain continued. Somewhere in the building, a
clock was ticking with the mechanical indifference of an object that does not
care what happens next.

Amateur hour. Vic Valentine — a licensed investigator, a man who had survived
the Western Front, a professional whose stock in trade was the careful
assessment of danger — had looked at Elbridge Ashburn's occult circle and
dismissed it as theater. And then he had vanished.

"His room at the hotel," Bill said. "It's still available?"

"Paid through end of May."

"Then we start there."

"Or," Sean said, "we go to Ashburn directly. If that's where Vic went
last —"

The debate was just beginning — the careful, circling negotiation of four
strangers with different instincts trying to find a direction they could all
walk in — when the door banged open.

A student stood in the frame. Young. White-faced. Rain-soaked. He was
breathing hard and his shirt was plastered to his chest and he looked at the
room full of strangers with the wild, unfocused eyes of a person carrying news
he does not want to deliver.

"Professor," he said. "Professor Fairchild."

"What is it?"

"Your friend. The detective. They found him."

The room went very still.

"Down by the river, sir. They pulled him from the water an hour ago."

Fairchild did not move.

"He's dead, Professor. I'm sorry. He's dead."

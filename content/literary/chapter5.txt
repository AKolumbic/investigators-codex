CHAPTER FIVE — THE ASHBURN BOOKS


                              I.
                       THE MEN AT THE WINDOW


The bartender's promise stayed in the room after he said it.

Whole town'll be drinking it soon.

There were sentences that behaved like smoke. They thinned, drifted, lost
shape, and became part of the general foulness of the air. This one did
not. It sat on the table with the empty glass and the damp rings and the
weak yellow light, solid as a knife.

Sean Murphy could still taste the liquor.

That was the worst of it. Not the warning. Not the cases brought in
through the back like any ordinary shipment. Not even the bartender's smug
little prophecy. The worst of it was that Sean's mouth remembered the
drink with the loyalty of a dog. Warm. Smooth. Too good. A thing made to
tell a man that his better judgment had always been an inconvenience.

Outside the dockside windows, Arkham had gone black and wet around the
edges. Rain made narrow mirrors of the street. Men hunched under hat brims
and moved quickly from one pool of lamplight to the next. The river smell
pressed at the door every time it opened: mud, iron, rot, and the old sour
breath of water that had carried too much away.

The men who delivered the cases had gone out again.

Sean was the first to move.

He crossed to the window fast enough that the nearest drinker flinched and
then pretended he had not. Sean looked through the rain-streaked glass,
expecting to catch the carriers loading themselves into a truck, turning
down an alley, fading into the ordinary traffic of men who did not want to
be followed.

They were standing across the street.

Not leaving. Not hurrying. Standing.

Two men under the weak light, coats dark with rain, faces turned toward the
window. Toward him. They had the stillness of men waiting for a train, or
for a verdict, or for someone else to make the first mistake.

Sean felt every old instinct in him step forward.

"They see me," he said.

Aiden looked up from the room. Nell turned. Mikel, who needed fewer words
than most men and trusted faces less than feet, had already begun watching
the street behind Sean's shoulder.

The men outside held Sean's gaze for one more second.

Then they turned and walked around the corner.

Sean was out the door before the room could decide whether to object.

The rain struck him full in the face. His shoes hit slick boards, then
pavement. He went after them with his bad shoulder tight and his right hand
ready to remember where the gun sat under his coat. At the corner he turned
hard, prepared for backs, for footsteps, for a car door slamming, for some
poor fool paid five dollars to get in his way.

There was nothing.

No men. No car pulling away. No open side door. No alley clutter disturbed
by a hurried passage. Just rain, brick, crates, a dead gas lamp, and the
long black seam of the street running empty toward the river.

Sean stood there, breathing hard.

Behind him, Mikel came up quiet. Aiden a moment later. Nell last, coat
pulled tight, eyes already searching for the detail that would make the
impossible smaller.

"Which way?" Aiden asked.

Sean did not answer immediately.

He had followed men before. Men who knew alleys, men who knew knives, men
who knew how to vanish into a crowd or a building or a waiting motorcar.
That was craft. This felt like a joke told by a room that did not care
whether he laughed.

"They went around the corner," Sean said.

Mikel moved ahead, checking doorways, mud, the narrow spaces between
buildings. He found no useful trail. The rain had done what rain always did
in Arkham: made the truth slippery and then congratulated itself for the
weather.

For a time they stood under the eaves and argued with what they had not
seen.

The Italians were the first explanation because they were the easiest. With
O'Bannion dead and his men cut down in public, the Italian outfits had the
most obvious motive. Territory did not stay ownerless. Liquor routes did
not remain unattended out of respect for the dead. A smart rival would move
fast.

Sean tried to make the delivery men fit that shape. The clothes, the faces,
the way they carried themselves, what kind of outfit would use men like
that and what kind would not. He found nothing firm enough to hold.

Mikel watched the corner more than the conversation. The words came too
fast for him, names and factions and guesses folding into one another. But
he understood absence. Men had been there. Then they had not. Whatever name
the others put to it, the shape of the thing was wrong.

"Maybe Italians," Sean said finally, with no conviction in it.

"Or maybe that's what everyone is supposed to say," Nell said.

Aiden glanced back toward the bar, toward the bottles and the cases and the
men pretending not to watch them. "O'Bannion's people are dead. The
Italians are the obvious story. Obvious stories are useful."

Sean spat rainwater into the gutter.

"Then who?"

No one answered at first.

They all knew the name. They had heard it in June St. Clair's warning, in
the dead man's mouth outside the Hotel Miskatonic, and now in the silent
space left by men who had delivered poison and disappeared too neatly for
ordinary muscle.

The King in Shadow.

The name did not explain anything. It did something worse. It arranged the
questions.


                              II.
                         ST. MARY'S AFTER DARK


They went back through the leads like men searching a coat for money they
had already spent.

The asylum was there, dark and waiting in Fr. Pawel's memory. The library
was there, with its restricted shelves and its dry guardian at the desk.
The docks were there all around them, smelling of the new liquor and the
old river. But the loudest lead remained the one that had been making noise
since the beginning: Ashburn, his social circle, his old family, his robed
fools, and the cemetery where Arkham kept finding signs it could pretend
not to understand.

So Sean drove.

The Studebaker took the wet streets with a hard, competent growl. Sean had
the wheel as if he were settling an argument with it. Mikel sat forward,
eyes measuring turns and grade and the wet geometry of Arkham's hills.
Every town made more sense to him when rendered as movement: street to
street, river to bridge, bridge to church, church to graveyard. Aiden
watched the mirrors. Nell watched the city.

St. Mary's Graveyard waited in Southside with the patience of old stone.

The rain had softened to a mist by then, low and silver in the lantern
light. Headstones leaned at angles that suggested either age or quiet
argument. Yews and bare-limbed trees made black shapes against the sky.
The place was not silent. No graveyard is. There was always the sound of
water in leaves, of shoes in wet grass, of distant motors, of the city
continuing elsewhere with the indecency of the living.

They left the car and entered carefully.

Nell kept her notebook away. Some places did not want paper. Aiden moved
like a man who had learned long ago that trespassing was mostly a matter of
confidence until the moment it was not. Sean scanned for silhouettes, for a
glint of metal, for anyone pretending to be more devout than frightened.
Mikel watched the ground.

It was Mikel who first sensed that the cemetery was not empty.

Or perhaps it was Aiden who caught the movement and Mikel who understood
it. Later neither version mattered. What mattered was the dark figure
between the stones, robe brushing wet grass, moving with the solemn hurry
of an amateur trying to look ancient.

They followed.

Beyond a scatter of older graves, where the stones were worn shallow by
weather and neglect, four figures stood with their arms raised. Robes.
Hoods. A little too clean in some places, muddy at the hems in others.
Their chant moved in uneven waves, syllables stretched and swallowed, the
cadence half church, half theater, and not enough of either to convince
God or the dead.

For a few breaths the investigators watched from the dark.

The scene should have been ridiculous.

It was ridiculous.

That did not make it harmless.

Harriet Stockbridge's warning returned with the damp and the candle stink:
You don't have to understand electricity to get electrocuted.

Sean shifted his weight. A wet branch snapped somewhere under someone's
shoe. One of the robed figures broke rhythm. Aiden moved. Mikel looked
left. Nell breathed in sharply.

The spell, if it had ever been one, collapsed into panic.

The cultists ran.

They did not scatter with discipline. They bolted like embarrassed boys
caught vandalizing a schoolhouse. One nearly slipped on the grass. Another
grabbed at his robe to keep from tripping over his own borrowed mystery.
Within seconds the chanting had become footfalls and then the footfalls had
gone among the trees.

What remained was mud, disturbed grass, cheap candles guttering in the
mist, and no sign that any great secret of the cosmos had been successfully
called down upon Southside.

Nell crouched near the place where they had stood. Sean checked the stones
for marks. Aiden looked for symbols, placement, pattern. Mikel searched the
ground for practical facts.

No glyphs. No blood. No serious arrangement of stones. No occult residue
that looked like anything more than effort, costume, and fear.

Then Nell found the paper.

A flyer, damp at the corner, folded once and trampled at the edge. The ink
had bled slightly but not enough to hide the words.

Ashburn Social Society.

Tonight.

Sean looked at it and then toward the trees where the cultists had gone.

"Subtle," he said.

The cultists had fled, but not far enough.

By the time the investigators returned to the road, figures were breaking
from the woods toward another car. Robes bunched in fists. Hoods down now.
Men breathing hard, stripped of ceremony by the simple need not to be
caught.

Sean reached the Studebaker first.

The engine turned over. The car rolled forward, smooth and sudden, and came
up near enough to the fleeing men that the nearest one made a small sound
that had no religion in it at all.

For one ridiculous second everyone stopped.

Then the cultists ran back into the woods.

Aiden watched them vanish between the trees. "That may be the least
convincing secret society in New England."

"It's still a secret," Nell said, folding the flyer into her notebook.
"They just seem bad at the society."

Mikel did not catch all of that, but he understood the smiles that did not
reach anyone's eyes.

The paper had given them a destination. Sean turned the car toward the
Ashburn estate.


                              III.
                         THE MAN IN THE DOORWAY


The Ashburn estate looked best from a distance.

That was true of many old fortunes in 1930. From the road, with trees
softening the edges and the night hiding repairs deferred too long, the
house still had the dignity of money that expected obedience. Close up, the
details betrayed it. Paint tired at the trim. Gravel thin in the drive.
Curtains good but old. The lawn kept presentable in the way a man keeps a
collar straight when the shirt beneath is fraying.

The Depression had not made the Ashburns poor. Not yet. It had made the
effort visible.

Sean parked where the car could watch without announcing itself too loudly.
This, like most compromises, fooled no one completely.

They waited.

The cultists' motorcar returned after a few hours, moving with the guilty
care of men who had practiced secrecy only in rooms where everyone already
agreed to pretend. Sean let several minutes pass, then followed them in.
The investigators stayed in the Studebaker while the meeting continued
inside.

There are few places more intimate than a car parked outside a house where
one has no legal business. Arguments become whispers. Every movement inside
the house seems meaningful. Every light in every window appears to know
something.

They debated what to do next.

Go in. Wait. Leave. Knock. Watch. Force the issue. Let the issue ripen.

Aiden favored pressure applied elegantly. Sean favored the kind that left a
mark. Nell favored information and did not care much whether the
information felt flattered. Mikel listened and watched the house, the drive,
the doors, the places people came and went.

At last the meeting ended without the investigators making the choice at
all.

Guests emerged from the house in coats and hats, talking too quietly and
too quickly. Respectable people, or people dressed as respectable people,
which in Arkham often served the same purpose. They got into their cars.
Engines coughed. Tires rolled over gravel. One by one the Ashburn Social
Society returned itself to the city it pretended not to resemble.

Elbridge Ashburn III remained in the doorway.

He stood framed by warm light and old wood, one hand resting lightly
against the jamb, smiling as if the night had finally remembered its host.
He was dressed well enough to be taken seriously by anyone who still
mistook fabric for character. His face had the pleasant, arranged quality
of a man forever waiting to be painted.

He looked directly at the Studebaker.

Then he lifted a hand and waved them in.

Sean muttered something unprintable.

Aiden smiled faintly. "Well. There's the invitation."

Inside, the house smelled of polish, extinguished candles, old paper, and
the lingering breath of guests who had spent the evening convincing
themselves they belonged to something larger than a parlor game.

Ashburn received them as if they had arrived for brandy rather than
accusation.

"Mr. Chez," he said, because Aiden had the sort of presence men like
Ashburn liked to identify first. Then his gaze moved to Nell, to Sean, to
Mikel, arranging them into whatever hierarchy made the evening most
interesting to him. "Miss Carroway. Gentlemen. I wondered how long you
would wait in the motorcar."

"You saw us," Nell said.

"My dear, one does not maintain a house like this by failing to notice
automobiles in the drive."

Sean did not sit.

None of them did at first.

They asked about the cemetery.

Ashburn lied.

Not skillfully. Not even with the practical talent of a man protecting a
crime. He lied like a man performing innocence for an audience he assumed
had paid for the better seats. He tilted his head. He frowned. He used
phrases such as "unfortunate enthusiasm" and "youthful misinterpretation,"
which might have served him better if the cultists in question had not
been grown men in robes running through wet woods like spooked clerks.

"I cannot possibly be accountable," Ashburn said, "for every excitable soul
in Arkham who finds symbolism more compelling than church attendance."

Nell's expression did not change. "But you know which excitable souls those
were."

"I know many people."

"You know those people."

Ashburn smiled with one side of his mouth.

Sean had seen better lies from men with broken noses and police at the
door.

But when Vic Valentine's name entered the room, Ashburn's performance
shifted.

Not much. Enough.

He blinked. His smile faltered, not theatrically but because the thing said
to him had arrived from outside the script.

"Mr. Valentine?" he said. "What of him?"

"He's dead," Aiden said.

For the first time that night, Elbridge Ashburn III looked genuinely
unprepared.

It did not make him innocent. Arkham was not so generous. But it separated
one kind of guilt from another. Ashburn knew the cemetery. Ashburn knew the
robes. Ashburn knew far more about his little society than he admitted.
But Vic's death was news to him, and news has a different face than
theater.

The conversation changed.

They pressed him about Vic. About Fairchild. About the family, the books,
the old Ashburn material Harriet had described with such sharp disgust.

There Ashburn became eager again, because vanity survives most forms of
danger. He spoke of legacy and rightful property, of misunderstood
inheritance, of university men growing possessive over what had never
belonged to them.

"I only wanted what is mine," he said.

"The books," Nell said.

"The Ashburn books," he corrected, too quickly.

Aiden leaned forward. "And Professor Fairchild helped you with that?"

Ashburn's eyes moved, just once, toward the empty fireplace.

It was the smallest admission possible. Then he made it larger by trying to
sound casual.

"I consulted Professor Fairchild, yes. A man of his academic standing
understands that families such as mine have obligations to their own
history."

The room seemed to narrow around the name.

Fairchild.

Helpful, tired, generous Professor Fairchild, who had sent them toward
Ashburn and omitted the part where Ashburn had already come to him about
restricted occult books.

Sean looked at Nell. Nell looked at Aiden. Mikel watched all three of them
and understood the shift even before the words settled.

Something Fairchild had not said had become louder than everything Ashburn
had.


                              IV.
                         THE DREAM IN THE WATER


Sleep came badly.

For Sean, it came like water.

At first he was back in the dockside bar. There was wood under his hands
and lamplight on the glass and the taste of the liquor still on his tongue.
Then the boards darkened. The room tipped. The glass became a black circle
of water and the water became the room and the room became the river.

He was drowning before he knew he had fallen.

Cold closed over him with the sudden intimacy of hands. There was no
surface, only the idea of one somewhere above and getting farther away. He
could see light through the water, thin and broken, like streetlamps
reflected in rain. His coat dragged at him. His shoulder burned. His lungs
began their old animal argument with the rest of him.

Something moved below.

Not fish. Not current. Something with patience.

The river opened under his feet into a depth no river in Massachusetts had
any right to possess. Shapes uncoiled there, pale where the darkness
touched them, then gone, then back again nearer than before. Tentacles,
though the word came too small and too late. They did not strike. They
waited until waiting became worse.

One brushed his ankle.

Then another closed around his leg.

Sean fought.

He had fought men in alleys, on docks, in rooms where the wallpaper smelled
of beer and blood. He had fought with fists, with guns, with cars, with the
kind of smile that told a man violence was already happening and he had not
yet noticed. None of it mattered in the water. Every movement became slow.
Every curse became bubbles. Every instinct came back empty-handed.

The dark below him was not simply dark.

It was looking up.

Then the thing pulled.

Sean woke with his hand at his throat and the taste still in his mouth.

Morning had not improved Arkham. It had only made the city visible again.

The investigators met at the Arkham Gazette, where the day's first sounds
were already becoming argument. Typewriters. Shoes on boards. Men clearing
their throats before lying into telephones. The smell of ink and coffee and
damp wool. Wallace Baines somewhere in the building, surely angry about
something and therefore alive.

Nell belonged there in a way the rest of them did not. The Gazette was not
safe, exactly. No place that printed truths beside advertisements for
miracle tonics and foreclosure notices could claim that. But it gave her a
desk, a language, and the useful illusion that chaos could be made smaller
by putting it in columns.

They spread the night out between them.

The delivery men. The vanished corner. The cemetery. The flyer. Ashburn's
doorway smile. His lies. His ignorance of Vic's death. His talk of the
family books.

And Fairchild.

That was where the line snagged.

"He never said," Nell said.

Aiden looked at her.

"Fairchild," she said. "He never said Ashburn came to him about the books."

Sean sat back. He looked tired in the hard way that came after too little
sleep and too vivid a dream. "Maybe he forgot."

No one believed that.

Mikel watched their faces. He did not need every word. Fairchild's name had
changed weight. Before, it had been a door. A safe office. A man with
answers. Now it had become a space in the record where something should
have been and was not.

Harriet Stockbridge had given them the next name days earlier, before they
understood how badly they needed it.

Armitage.

Dr. Henry Armitage, guardian of the Orne Library and the sort of man who
probably considered locks a charitable alternative to incineration.

So they went back to the university.


                              V.
                     THE BOOKS BEHIND THE DESK


The Orne Library looked different in daylight.

Not safer. Only more official.

Students moved through the stacks with books under their arms and private
disasters on their faces. Professors crossed the main floor as if late to
meetings nobody could afford to make useful. Rain tapped the high windows
with the bored persistence of a clerk denied promotion. Everything smelled
of paper, wool, floor wax, and old caution.

At the center of it stood Dr. Henry Armitage.

He had the look of a man who had outlived several intellectual fashions and
intended to outlive several more by refusing to be impressed. His spectacles
sat low. His expression sat lower. Behind him, the library rose in tiers
and shadows, and somewhere beyond ordinary reach were the shelves that had
drawn Vic Valentine, Elbridge Ashburn, Professor Fairchild, and now all of
them into the same narrowing corridor.

Aiden spoke first because Aiden made refusal work harder for a living.

He gave Armitage enough truth to make the request serious and not enough to
make it foolish. Vic Valentine. The Ashburn family. Harriet Stockbridge's
research. The restricted material. A pattern of harm moving through Arkham
that did not yet have a respectable explanation.

Armitage listened.

He had a way of listening that made interruption seem like something other
people did before they learned shame.

When Aiden finished, Armitage folded his hands.

"The restricted section," he said, "is not a municipal curiosity."

"Nobody said it was," Sean said.

Armitage's eyes moved to him. "No. But many men have implied it by the
quality of their requests."

Sean smiled without warmth. "I can make mine worse if that'll help."

Nell stepped in before that could become the kind of exchange libraries
remembered unfavorably.

"A man is dead," she said. "The books may be part of why."

That changed the room a little. Not enough to open a door. Enough to make
the hinges think about it.

Armitage knew Vic's name. Or perhaps he knew the type of man Vic had been:
Boston suit, tired eyes, questions arranged too carefully to be casual. He
did not say so at once. Men like Armitage preferred information to arrive
with invoices.

"Mr. Valentine came here," Aiden said.

The old scholar's expression did not break, but something moved behind it.

Nell saw it. Sean saw Nell see it. Mikel saw the chain of seeing and leaned
closer to the table, because sometimes language was less important than the
direction in which silence turned.

"He asked about the Ashburn books," Nell said.

Armitage removed his spectacles and cleaned them with a cloth so precise it
might have been a ritual.

"Many people have asked about the Ashburn books," he said. "Most of them
should not have known enough to ask."

"Did Fairchild?" Aiden asked.

There it was.

The name lay on the desk between them.

For the first time, Armitage looked less like a gate and more like a man
standing in front of one while listening to footsteps on the other side.

"Professor Fairchild," he said, "is a member of this university."

"That isn't an answer," Nell said.

"It is the answer most likely to preserve my employment."

"Did he have the books?"

Armitage put his spectacles back on.

Outside, a car passed through standing water. Somewhere in the stacks, a
student coughed. The entire library seemed, for one narrow second, to be
waiting for permission to breathe.

"Professor Fairchild checked out the Ashburn materials," Armitage said.

Sean's face did not change. That was how Nell knew the answer had landed
hard.

"When?" Aiden asked.

Armitage did not give them the ledger. He did not give them the restricted
section. He did not give them the satisfaction of believing he had been won
over. But he gave them enough.

Fairchild had taken the books.

Vic Valentine had come four or five days earlier asking after those same
books.

And Vic had come from Arkham Asylum.

The asylum again.

The word moved through the group like cold air under a door. Fr. Pawel's
unease. The old case that was not possession. Vic following the trail from
that place to these books. Fairchild already in possession of them. Ashburn
claiming he had consulted Fairchild about retrieving them. Fairchild saying
nothing.

"Did Vic know Fairchild had them?" Nell asked.

Armitage looked at her for a long moment. Perhaps weighing the question.
Perhaps deciding whether the truth was safer in his keeping or more
dangerous there.

"No," he said.

One word. Dry as paper. Heavy as stone.

Aiden thanked him. Nell did not. Sean looked as if he wanted a drink and
hated himself for it. Mikel watched the library shelves, the old scholar,
the door, and his friends, and knew even without all the words that they
had found something important enough to make everyone quieter.

They left without the books.

But they left with the order of things.

Fairchild first.

Vic after.

The asylum before the library.

And somewhere behind those facts, still faceless and patient, the King in
Shadow waited for Arkham to tell itself another smaller story than the
truth.
